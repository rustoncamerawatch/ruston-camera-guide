01

What this ticket actually is

Plain facts first — no scare tactics, in either direction.

A civil penalty, not a criminal ticket

Ruston's cameras are run with vendor NovoaGlobal on behalf of the Ruston Police Department. When the system records a speed violation, the city says a Ruston officer reviews it before a Notice of Infraction (NOI) is mailed to the vehicle's registered owner — whether or not that person was driving.

Under Washington law these camera infractions are civil penalties, handled much like parking tickets. They do not add points to a driver's license, and they are not reported to insurance companies.

The fine is a flat $145 for any violation of 6 mph or more over the posted limit, in both regular speed zones and park zones. 31 in a 25 costs the same as 45 in a 25.

The one deadline that matters is printed on your notice: respond by the due date on your NOI. Paying, contesting, or asking for a mitigation hearing all count as responses — ignoring it does not make it go away. If you missed a deadline or a hearing, the city says to contact the Court Clerk about a Good Cause Hearing Request.
02

Your options — all three of them

The notice gives you choices. Paying is only one, and it's the only one that costs the city nothing.

Option 1

Pay it

$145

You admit nothing in court, but the money is gone and the violation stands. Pay online through NovoaGlobal's ZeroFatality portal using the plate number and password on your notice, or by check/money order as directed on the notice. Card payments carry a processing fee.

This is what the program is designed for you to do. Every paid-without-a-fight ticket is pure margin for the vendor model.

Option 2 — make them work

Contest it

Hearing

A contested hearing means you say the ticket was issued in error, and the city has to present its evidence to a judge. You can present evidence too — photos, records, the camera's basis for being there at all.

Request it through the ZeroFatality portal listed on your notice, before the due date. Hearings are held at the Mary Joyce Community Center, 5219 N Shirley St, Ruston. You'll be mailed your hearing date.

Option 3

Mitigation hearing

Ask for less

You agree the violation happened but explain the circumstances and ask the judge to reduce the fine. The violation remains on the record either way.

If the issue is money, the city also offers a payment plan and a reduced penalty for people on state public assistance (TANF, Apple Health/Medicaid, SSI, SNAP) or WIC — both go through the Court Clerk, and a reduced penalty also requires requesting a mitigation hearing.

How to request a hearing, step by step

  1. Find the due date on your Notice of Infraction. That's your deadline — calendar it now. Every step below has to happen by that date.
  2. Decide: contested or mitigation. Contested = “this was issued in error, prove it.” Mitigation = “it happened, but here's why the fine should be lower.” You can't argue both at once — pick the one that fits.
  3. Submit the request. The city directs hearing requests through NovoaGlobal's ZeroFatality portal (plate number + password from your notice). You can also put it in writing to the court — the letter builder below generates exactly that letter, which you can mail or hand-deliver so you have a paper trail.
  4. Watch the mail for your hearing date. Ruston traffic hearings are at the Mary Joyce Community Center, 5219 N Shirley St. If no response arrives by your due date, call the Court Clerk: (253) 759-3544 x106 · TrafficHearings@RustonWA.gov.
  5. Show up (or send a written statement). The city also offers an “eHearing” — a written statement a judge reviews instead of an in-person appearance — but note: decisions on written statements cannot be appealed. If the car wasn't yours to answer for (sold, stolen, or in someone else's care at the time), file a Declaration of Non-Responsibility through the portal instead.

04

Yes, there are other cameras

The 51st Street pair is the one everyone talks about. It is not the whole program.

The City's own camera page lists five cameras in three areas, live since October 3, 2026 after a 30-day warning period. The vendor's September 2, 2026 launch release groups them the same way. Every location below is from those two sources — linked at the bottom of this page.

5400 block, N 51st Street

EASTBOUND + WESTBOUND · 2 CAMERAS

City's stated basis: the “Winnifred Park Area” — a public park speed zone. The cameras face both directions along the park's northern edge.

Question to press: state law defines a public park speed zone as the marked area within park property and extending 300 feet from its border, “consistent with active park use,” with signs posted. Winnifred Park is a wooded greenbelt strip with one small field (see the satellite photo in §01). Whether that is “active park use” — and whether the city's pre-authorization location analysis actually said so — is exactly what the pending records request asks for.

4900–5000 block, N Baltimore Street

NORTHBOUND (5000 BLK) + SOUTHBOUND (4900 BLK) · 2 CAMERAS

City's stated basis: the “Rust Park Area” — again a public park speed zone, per the vendor's launch release. The city's camera page lists the two Baltimore Street views separately, at the 5000 block northbound and the 4900 block southbound.

Question to press: the same statutory test — within 300 feet of the park border, consistent with active park use, and signed as a park speed zone. Ask for the location analysis for this site specifically, not just the program-wide study.

4900 block, N Pearl Street

NORTHBOUND ONLY · 1 CAMERA

City's stated basis: the launch release calls North Pearl a “high-risk corridor” and groups it under “high-risk traffic areas.” No park, school, or hospital is named for this location.

Flagged — the weakest stated justification: “high-risk corridor” is not one of the location categories listed for speed cameras in RCW 46.63.250(2). The current statute leaves two possible fits — a designated crash prevention zone, or the separate provision allowing one additional camera per 10,000 residents at a location the council deems higher-crash-risk from speeding — but neither basis has been stated publicly for Pearl Street in the sources above. That doesn't prove the camera is unlawful; it means the city hasn't shown its work. The ordinance and the location analysis, once produced, either answer this or they don't.

What state law actually allows

One wrinkle worth knowing: the city and vendor materials cite RCW 46.63.170. That section was repealed in 2024 and replaced by RCW 46.63.210–.260. Speed cameras for locations like Ruston's now sit in RCW 46.63.250, which permits speed enforcement only in:

  • Hospital speed zones
  • Public park speed zones
  • School speed zones
  • School walk zones
  • Crash prevention zones
  • Roadway work zones (only while workers are present)
  • State highways within city limits classified as city streets

Plus, under RCW 46.63.250(3), one additional camera per 10,000 residents at a higher-crash-risk location the local legislative authority designates. Definitions — including “public park speed zone” and its “consistent with active park use” condition — are in RCW 46.63.210.

What we could not verify

Honest limits: the full text of Ordinance 1599's location findings, the traffic/speed studies behind each site, the camera calibration records, and the city's revenue split with NovoaGlobal are not public yet — they are the subject of the records requests in §06, which the city estimates could take up to a year to produce.

This page lists only the five camera views the city itself publishes. If the city posts additional locations, this list should be updated from that source — not from rumor.

Vendor claim, for context: the launch release says traffic studies found about 14% of the roughly 4 million vehicles passing through Ruston each year exceed “established safety speed thresholds.” The underlying studies have not been produced yet, so treat the number as the vendor's, not a verified fact.

05

Nobody voted on this

Not a ballot measure. Never was going to be.

How it actually happened

Washington law puts this decision entirely in the hands of the local legislative authority: do a location analysis, pass an ordinance, post signs. No resident vote is required, and none was held.

Ruston's council took up Ordinance 1599 — “Adopting RMC Ch. 16.05 Establishing Automated Traffic Safety Cameras” — at its second reading on February 17, 2026, at the Mary Joyce Community Center. On that same agenda sat Resolution 819, authorizing the agreement with NovoaGlobal, Inc. The program the ordinance created was announced September 2, 2026 and began issuing fines October 3, 2026.

Five councilmembers and a mayor, in a town of roughly a thousand people, switched this on. That's legal. It's also the whole argument: nobody asked you.

Why that cuts both ways

What a council can pass by ordinance, a council can repeal by ordinance — no election, no ballot measure, no waiting for Olympia. Removal runs through the same room where this started.

  • Public comment at regular council meetings, Mary Joyce Community Center, 5219 N Shirley St.
  • The paper trail — every records installment (§06) is material for comment, letters, and press.
  • The contract — repeal pressure lands hardest at renewal or termination points in the NovoaGlobal agreement, which is why getting the contract itself is priority #1 in the records fight.
  • Contested hearings — a program that costs the city court time on ticket after ticket is a program whose math stops working (§02).
06

The public record so far

What's been asked for, what the city said, and what's still owed. Dates are from the correspondence itself.

  1. OCT 21, 2026

    Public Records Request filed (PRR 26-108)

    Asks for the NovoaGlobal contract and revenue split, monthly revenue reports, vendor invoices and city payments, citation statistics by location, calibration and maintenance records, traffic/speed studies, authorizing ordinances and minutes, and the implementation/warning-period timeline.

  2. OCT 22, 2026

    City classifies it “Complex” — up to one year

    Ruston logged the request as PRR 26-108, classified it “Complex,” and estimated completion could take up to one year, with installments every 30 days and the first installment due November 30, 2026. Standard RCW 42.56.120 fees apply; the request caps pre-approval spending at $25.

  3. OCT 22, 2026

    Appeal + pushback sent

    An appeal of the “Complex” classification went to Mayor Bruce Hopkins — filed on the city's NextRequest thread and duplicated by email to the City Clerk the same day, inside the 10-business-day appeal window. It objects to the one-year estimate, accepts the 30-day installments, and sets a priority order: contract first, then revenue reports, invoices/payments, citation stats, remainder last.

  4. OCT 22, 2026

    Second, standalone request: the procurement file

    A separate Public Records Act request for how NovoaGlobal got the job — solicitation/RFP records, competing proposals, evaluation and scoring records, any sole-source justification, and city–vendor communications from January 2024 through contract execution. It runs on its own five-business-day acknowledgment clock.

  5. ONGOING · NEXT CHECK NOV 1, 2026

    Monthly follow-ups — updating records only

    Follow-ups ask only for records that change: new monthly revenue reports, new invoices and payments, new citation statistics. One-time items (the contract, calibration records, studies, ordinances) are asked once and never re-requested once produced. New categories can be added at any time.

  6. DUE NOV 30, 2026

    First installment deadline

    The date to hold the city to. A late or empty first installment is what turns a slow response into a story — and into grounds for the free Attorney General Open Government Ombuds route and, ultimately, RCW 42.56.550.

No numbers yet, on purpose. This page does not publish ticket counts or revenue figures for Ruston, because none have been produced or verified. When the installments land, the real numbers go here — with the documents behind them.
